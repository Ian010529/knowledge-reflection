According to thermodynamic theory, Se is associated with the solvation-structural
entropy difference (DS) and the concentration ratio difference (DCr) between redox
species.8 Strategies for enhancing either DS or DCr therefore enable the enhance-
ment of Se. It has been observed that DS is related to factors that include the abso-
lute charges and complex structures of redox species and their surrounding solvent
and solute species in electrolytes.7,29 In general, redox species with a high absolute
charge and complicated complex structure possess a large DS and thus have a high
Se For example, the Se of [Fe(CN)6]4/[Fe(CN)6]3 (1.4 mV K1) is much higher
than that of Fe2+/Fe3+ (1.0 mV K1). Furthermore, using specific solvents and sol-
utes to rearrange the solvation structures of redox species is a more effective
approach to enhance Se (Figure 3A). Pringle et al. reported a series of high-Se
(maximum value 2.65 mV K1) thermocells in non-aqueous electrolytes (pure ionic
liquid or ionic liquid mixture).14,30–34 Kim et al. added methanol to [Fe(CN)6]4/
[Fe(CN)6]3 aqueous electrolytes and achieved a 2-fold enhancement of Se from
1.4 to 2.9 mV K1 because the methanol selectively rearranged the solvation
structure of [Fe(CN)6]4.16 Our previous research reported an unprecedented high
Se of 4.2 mV K1 by introducing urea and guanidinium into [Fe(CN)6]4/[Fe(CN)6]3
aqueous electrolytes.9 The results reveal that guanidinium and urea synergistically

presented a supramolecular methodology based on the encapsulation of I3 in

induced [Fe(CN)6]4 to form highly thermosensitive crystals on the cold side, while
the [Fe(CN)6]4-associated crystals redissolved on the hot side. As a result, a large

of these parameters, a record-high hr of 11.1% was achieved.

In general, the electrical conductivity of liquid-state thermocells is approximately
three orders of magnitude lower than that of traditional solid-state thermoelectric
cells. Therefore, early studies regarding improving the efficiency of liquid-state ther-
mocells mainly focused on enhancing electrical conductivity.11,35–37 As mentioned
previously, the electrical conductivity of liquid-state thermocells is related to the re-
action and transport kinetics of the redox species on the electrodes and in the elec-
trolytes. seff is a comprehensive electrical conductivity parameter that is limited by
three primary internal resistances: activation, ohmic, and mass transport resistance.
The activation resistance originates from the activation barrier of reactions at the
electrodes. The ohmic resistance is the series resistances of the electrode and elec-
trolyte. The mass transport resistance is associated with ion diffusion and convection

at the electrode-electrolyte interface and in the bulk electrolyte. Therefore,
improving either the electrolytes or electrodes enables the enhancement of seff.
Regarding electrolyte improvement, enhancing the solubility of redox species in
electrolytes has been proven to be an effective way to decrease ohmic resistance38;
however, the strategy may increase the mass transport resistance because of an in-
crease in the viscosity of the electrolyte. In addition, some redox species possess the
concentration-dependent thermopower, and their Se substantially decays at a
higher concentration. One example is that the Se of I/I3 decreases by over 4-
fold with an increase in concentration from 0.01 to 2 M.39 In comparison, improving
the electrode can independently enhance seff without sacrificing Se; thus, more ad-
vances have been achieved by electrode improvement. In early studies, Pt elec-
trodes with high catalytic activity and fast electron transfer were examined in ther-
mocells.10,40,41 Recent studies have proven that carbon nanomaterials (e.g.,
carbon nanotubes and graphene) with electrochemical activity similar to that of Pt
are low-cost alternatives as electrode materials. Moreover, carbon nanomaterials
provide a high gravimetric surface area and are thus capable of significantly
enhancing the current density and overall efficiency. Baughman et al. reported a
high hr of 1.4% by using multi-walled carbon nanotubes as electrodes.11 Chen
et al. raised hr to 2.6% by introducing a single-walled carbon nanotube/reduced gra-
phene oxide composite electrode.12 Kim et al. further boosted hr to 3.95% by utiliz-
ing an aligned carbon nanotube aerogel sheet with Pt nanoparticles deposited as
electrodes.13

In contrast to improving Se and seff, suppressing keff was ignored in early studies.
One important reason is that the keff of thermocells is difficult to measure using clas-
sical transient methods (e.g., hot-wire or laser flash methods). Typically, the transient
hot-wire method only measures the intrinsic thermal conduction of liquids.42 In fact,
thermal convection is not negligible when a temperature gradient is built in a
liquid.43,44 Even keff is dominated by thermal convection under a large temperature
difference. Current studies have indicated that a steady-state method is more reli-
able than transient methods for measuring the keff of liquid thermocells.8,45 More
importantly, suppressing thermal convection has gradually been realized to be an
effective approach to enhance thermocell efficiency. Baughman et al. reported
that filling thermal separators in electrolytes enables a significant enhancement of
thermocell power density.38 They noted that thermal separators with appropriate
thickness and porosity enabled an increase of temperature difference of 20 C by
suppressing the thermal convection of the electrolytes, thereby achieving a high po-
wer density of 12 W m2. Hasan et al. used a poly(vinylidene fluoride) porous mem-
brane as a thermal separator to suppress thermal convection, which also boosted the
performance of thermocells.46 Another strategy for suppressing thermal convection
is using gel-based electrolytes in thermocells. With the quasi-solid-state feature,
thermal convection is negligible in gel-based electrolytes. Liu et al. reported a low
thermal conductivity of 0.15 W m1 K1 in a gelatin hydrogel-based electrolyte.17
Chen et al. observed a low thermal conductivity of 0.3–0.4 W m1 K1 in a poly-
acrylamide hydrogel-based electrolyte.47

Device integration and applications
In addition to improving the efficiency of single cells, another research hotspot fo-
cuses on device integration and applications.19,20,48 Because the voltage output
of a single cell is limited by the thermopower and the temperature difference, inte-
grating multiple cells in a series is needed to generate a useful voltage (>1 V) for
practical applications. As shown in Figure 4, there are two kinds of integration
methods: Z-shaped connections of one type of single cell in a series, and P-shaped

connections of two types (n-type and p-type) of single cells in a series. For thermo-
cells, one with an oxidation reaction happening on the hot electrode is commonly
defined as p-type, otherwise, one with an reduction reaction happening on the
hot electrode is defined as n-type.49,50 One advantage of the Z-shaped connection
is that the corresponding device can achieve a maximum power output by using
state-of-the-art single cells (Figure 4A). Our current research demonstrated a mod-
ule based on a Z-shaped connection of 20 units (thermosensitive crystallization-
boosted thermocells with a Se of 3.73 mV K1), which generated an open-circuit
voltage (Voc) of 3.1 V and a high maximum power density (Pmax) of 6.86 W m2 under
a temperature difference of 50 C.8 Due to this excellent performance, the module
was successfully used to charge a mobile smartphone. However, Z-shaped connec-
tions complicate the device integration process and possibly introduce large contact
resistance between the electrodes and Z-shaped wires. In contrast, P-shaped con-
nections simplify fabrication and enable good contact between the electrodes
and current collectors (Figure 4B). Baughman et al. demonstrated a module inte-
grating 14 p-type ([Fe(CN)6]4/[Fe(CN)6]3) cells and 14 n-type (Fe2+/Fe3+) cells by
P-shaped connection. Four modules in the series generated a Voc of 2.18 V under
a temperature difference of 21 C, and the system was used to charge capaci-
tances.38 Note that due to the lack of high-performance n-type cells, devices inte-
grated by P-shaped connection remain inefficient.

In addition, device integration faces the challenge of liquid leakage, which is more
serious for flexible thermocells. To this end, leak-free gel-based devices have
been proposed for human body heat harvesting.19,51 Our previous work introduced
a wearable thermocell based on poly(vinyl alcohol) hydrogel electrolytes. This de-
vice, integrating 59 cell pairs in a series, enables a maximum voltage of 0.7 V by uti-
lizing body heat.19 Chen et al. used the same strategy to fabricate an advanced hy-
drogel-based thermocell with optimized electrodes and electrolytes.20 They utilized
this thermocell to demonstrate a proof-of-concept watch strap that could harvest
body heat, charge a supercapacitor, and power a green light-emitting diode. Pringle
et al. developed a leak-free, non-volatile, and flexible thermocell by using ionic
liquid-based organogels as electrolytes.21 They further demonstrated the organo-
gel-based thermocell enabled a stable device for low-grade heat harvesting.22

Regarding applications, current studies have demonstrated that thermocells can not
only harvest heat independently but also enable integration with other systems to
achieve synergistic enhancement effects.52 Ho et al. proposed a water-energy har-
vesting system integrating a photothermal evaporator and a thermocell, which sup-
pressed heat losses, achieving a high solar-thermal conversion efficiency of 91.4%

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
With a growing thirst for low-grade heat energy harvesting, liquid-state thermo-
cells have gained increasing research attention because of their potential cost-
effectiveness, flexibility, and scalability for directly converting heat into electricity.
Despite the significant advances achieved by the optimization of electrolytes, elec-
trodes, thermal separators, and device modules, many critical challenges and
future research directions of liquid-state thermocells still need to be carefully
considered.


Continuously enhancing the efficiency of single cells
Compared with intensively studied solid-state thermoelectric cells, liquid-state ther-
mocells remain inefficient. With the well-known intrinsic interdependence of Se, seff,
and keff, achieving high efficiency of liquid-state thermocells is challenging. For
example, regulating the solvation structure of redox species enables the enhance-
ment of Se but is always associated with a decrease in seff because of the low ionic
conductivity in the optimized electrolytes. Utilizing a thermal separator can suppress
keff but also decrease seff. Although optimizing electrodes to improve reaction ki-
netics can independently boost seff without sacrificing Se and keff, the continued
enhancement of efficiency has encountered the limitation of thermodynamics. To
this end, future efforts should be focused on synergistic optimization of electrolytes,
electrodes, and thermal separators to decouple these three parameters, and there-
fore continuously enhance the efficiency of single cells.

Regarding the optimization of electrolytes, exploring new redox species with inher-
ently high Se values and high solubility has been recognized as an effective approach
to enhance efficiency. The desired redox couple may be designed based on two
principles: (1) a large absolute-charge density difference between oxidized and
reduced species of the redox couple29, and (2) weak interactions between redox
ions and their counterions.57 Typically, the benchmark system of K3Fe(CN)6/
K4Fe(CN)6 has a high Se of 1.4 mV K1 but a moderate solubility of 0.4 mol
L1; however, its solubility can be substantially increased by replacing the counter
K+ ions with NH4+ or Li+ ions.38,58 Second, developing new strategies to enhance
Se without sacrificing seff and keff is believed to be another way to enhance efficiency.

For example, thermal-sensitive crystallization has been proven to enable simulta-
neous enhancement of Se and suppression of keff, without sacrificing seff.8

The optimization of electrodes by using carbon-based nanomaterials with high spe-
cific surface areas has achieved significant advances in the last decade. However,
this strategy now encounters the bottleneck for continuously boosting efficiency
due to the reasons including the reaction thermodynamic limit (voltage) and un-
cleared reaction kinetic mechanisms on the scale of atoms and molecules. Two
fundamental questions involving in reaction kinetic mechanisms are needed to
answer. First, regarding the adsorption and desorption process: what is the profile
of the redox species that is distributed in the electric double layer of electrodes?
Second, regarding the charge transfer process: which kind of active sites (defect,
function group, or dopant) on carbon-based electrodes enable the promotion of
charge transfer? Therefore, insightfully understanding the microscale reaction ki-
netics of redox couples on electrodes (e.g., electric double layer, active site) by in
situ measurement and computational modeling is needed to direct improvement
of electrodes. On the other hand, the concept of asymmetric electrodes is ignored
in previous studies. Considered the reverse reaction occurring on the hot and cold
electrodes, designing asymmetric electrodes with selective adsorption properties
and oxidation/reduction catalytic activity may be an effective way to further improve
reaction kinetics. In addition, despite carbon nanomaterial electrodes being
cheaper than noble metal electrodes, their cost still accounts for a high proportion
of the total cost of single cells. Therefore, future efforts should use much cheaper
materials and milder synthesis methods to prepare electrodes for practical
applications.

Regarding the optimization of thermal separators, designing membranes with highly
ordered porous structures is beneficial for minimizing the sacrifice of ionic conduc-
tivity in electrolytes. 3D printing, as a fast and large-scale manufacturing technology,
is a promising method to produce thermal separators with ordered porous struc-
tures.59 Replacing liquid electrolytes with gel-based electrolytes is another way to
decrease keff; however, this approach also sacrifices seff due to the low ionic migra-
tion speed in quasi-solid-state electrolytes. Therefore, exploring new gel-based
electrolytes with high mass diffusion rates is also urgent, especially for wearable
thermocells.60 Pringle et al. investigated the incorporation of ionic liquids within
the hydrogel network as an effective route to enhance the electrochemical proper-
ties of gel-based thermocells.23 Zwitterionic gel-based electrolytes with high ionic
conductivity may be good candidates for wearable thermocells.60,61 In addition,
the thermodiffusion effect (also known as the Soret effect) also exists in the thermo-
cells.62,63 However, the thermodiffusion effect is commonly ignored in thermocells
due to its much smaller thermopower (on the order of 10 mV K1).64 Recently, Liu
et al. reported a giant thermopower (17 mV K1) in a gelatin electrolyte attributed
to a remarkable synergic effect between thermogalvanic and huge thermodiffusion
effects.17 This work provides a new strategy to boost performances of wearable
thermocells.

Improving the power output and stability of integrated devices
For practical applications, thermocells must integrate multiple p-type and n-type
legs in a series to achieve a high voltage (>1 V). Therefore, both p-type and n-
type units with high performance are necessary for devices. However, in contrast
to the significant advances in p-type thermocells, such as the [Fe(CN)6]4/
[Fe(CN)6]3 system, their n-type counterparts have significantly fallen behind. Typi-
cally, n-type species, including I/I3 and sulfate (Fe2+/Fe3+) redox couples, are

inefficient due to sluggish reaction kinetics.39,65 Therefore, developing highly effi-
cient n-type thermocells is crucial for device optimization. Current research has
proved that a perchlorate (Fe2+/Fe3+) redox couple exhibits a high positive Se of
1.76 mV K1 and high solubility (>1 M) in aqueous electrolytes due to the weak inter-
action between perchlorate anions and Fe2+/Fe3+ cations.49,50 This high-perfor-
mance n-type redox couple is worth a more thorough investigation. In addition,
the thermosensitive crystallization strategy,8 which highly boosted the performance
of K4Fe(CN)6/K3Fe(CN)6 system, also has great potential to be popularized to n-type
thermocells.

Another concern is the stability of devices under a wide temperature operation win-
dow. First, at high temperatures, devices will suffer a high risk of liquid leakage,
causing volume expansion of the electrolyte. In particular, aqueous electrolytes
exhibit 4% volume expansion when the temperature increases from 10 C to
90 C; liquid leakage would occur if the electrolyte is packaged in a rigid cell with
a low thermal expansion coefficient. Packing electrolytes with stretchable elasto-
mers (such as polydimethylsiloxane) or utilizing gel-based electrolytes should be
effective methods to address the problem of liquid leakage. In addition, aqueous
electrolyte-based thermocells also suffer volatility and boiling at high temperatures.
In this regard, ionic liquids with negligible volatility and good thermal and electro-
chemical stability are potential alternatives for water to extend the operation tem-
perature range (>100 C) of thermocells. Second, at low temperature, devices will
face the problem of crystallization of redox species and solvents, which could cause
a significant decrease in the ionic conductivity in the electrolytes. Adding cryopro-
tectants (e.g., inorganic salts, alcohols) to electrolytes is a promising approach to
expand the cold-temperature operation window.66 In addition, for wearable gel-
based devices, unstable contact between the hydrogel and electrodes is a challenge
for human body heat harvesting. Chemical bonding of gels on electrodes by
coupling agents is an effective way to improve the stability of wearable thermocell
devices.67

indirectly quantified by Z = (Se)2s/k, but this figure of merit is not suitable to evaluate

mocell is a whole device, and its electrical and thermal conductivity depends not
only on the electrolytes and electrodes but also on the operation conditions. Alter-
natively, the Carnot-relative efficiency (hr) is commonly used to directly evaluate the
performance of thermocells.8,13

power density (Pmax/(DT)2) has also been used to evaluate the performances of
thermocells.9,16

Although the temperature-independent feature, Pmax/(DT)2 is related with the

Therefore, a detailed test condition (at least including temperatures and device
sizes) is needed to list when comparing the performances of various thermocells
by these metrics. In future research, a normalized performance evaluation standard
must be established through joint efforts, which is significant to the development of
the whole thermocell field.

Expanding application fields of thermocell devices
Theoretically, thermocells enable power generation from all heat sources, while their
practical applications are significantly limited. One critical problem is that waste heat
is usually generated as a harmful by-product that primarily needs to be dissipated,
such as waste heat from solar panels, data centers, and various electronic de-
vices.68,69 If thermocells are directly attached to those sources, their low thermal
conductivity will suppress heat dissipation and further cause overheating of the
heat sources. Therefore, typical static-state thermocell devices are only suited to
harvest low-grade heat from sources (such as sunlight, the human body, and exhaust
pipes) that do not need heat dissipation. Alternatively, flow thermocells may be an
ideal choice to simultaneously scavenge and remove heat to maintain heat sources
in the operating range. Cola et al. first validated that flow thermocells have the ability
to simultaneously harvest waste heat and cooling; an optimized flow thermocell
generated a power density of 0.05 W m2 and achieved a heat transfer of 450 W
(m2 K)1 in parallel.70 They further enhanced the performances of flow thermocells
by using nanostructured carbon electrodes.71 With the unique advantage, flow ther-
mocells should be considered in future research. In addition, given the inherently
high thermopower of thermocells, potential applications of thermocells in the fields
of temperature detection and cooling are worth investigating further. On the aspect
of temperature detection, gel-based thermocells as soft temperature sensors are
promising for applications in electronic skin, human activity monitoring, personal
healthcare, and human-machine interface. On the aspect of cooling, we propose
further enhancing of a cooling effect is enabled by three strategies: (1) using redox
couples with high thermopower and high solubility, (2) suppressing parasitic Joule
heat by decreasing the electric resistance of the whole cell, and (3) expanding the
applied voltage range by suppressing the water electrolysis or using ionic liquids
with good electrochemical stability as solvents.
